Reading Slowly on a Machine Built for Speed

Typography is a voice you hear with your eyes. On the web — where nobody turns the page and everybody keeps score — every measure, margin, and em dash has to earn its keep.

JW
Juniper Hale
Type Director · March 14, 2025 · 18 min read
Now reading I. The Voice in the Machine 0%

There is a moment, just before the eyes settle, when a page decides whether it will be read. Not skimmed — read. The verdict is issued before a single word lands, by everything that is not the words: the width of the line, the weight of the ink against the field behind it, the small typographic courtesies that tell the eye where it may rest. Most pages on the web lose this argument in the first three seconds. They were built to be scrolled, and they read like it.

Typography is the voice you hear with your eyes. Set the same sentence in two different faces and you have two different sentences — one mumbled into a lapel microphone, one delivered from a stage with good acoustics. Print worked this out centuries ago and wrote it down; the craft has a spine, and its vertebrae are contrast, rhythm, and restraint.

The screen changed the substrate but not the task. A monitor is only paper that glows and forgets its margins. Everything the old craft knew — the measure, the baseline, the patient architecture of a well-built paragraph — still applies; it simply has to be re-argued in a medium that never sits still. This essay is that re-argument, at length, on purpose.

II.The Measure of a Line

The single most consequential number in reading typography is the one nobody notices: how many characters fit on a line. Typesetters call it the measure, and the comfortable range has been stable for roughly five hundred years — somewhere between forty-five and seventy-five characters, with sixty-six as the classical ideal.1

“Typography exists to honor content.”— Robert Bringhurst

Reading is a physical act, and the measure is a fact about the body, not about taste. The eye does not glide along a line; it leaps in jumps — saccades — pausing four or five times to take in a phrase, then making the return sweep: the small ballistic jump back to the left that must find the start of the next line. Make the line too long and the return sweep overshoots or undershoots; the eye lands mid-sentence and quietly re-reads a clause it has already paid for. Make it too short and the eye is interrupted every half-second, bounced down the page like a pinball. Somewhere in between, the machinery disappears, and you get the sensation people describe as “just reading.”On a phone the measure collapses to roughly 39 ch. The honest fix is not smaller type but shorter sentences — and paragraphs that end before the thumb tires.

This page is set at sixty-six characters, and it holds. Resize the window and the type bends a little as the viewport widens, but the line never stretches past what the return sweep can survive.3 That is the whole trick: not responsive decoration, but a promise the layout keeps to your eyes, every frame, without being asked.

III.Rhythm, or: Whitespace Is a Verb

If the measure is the room a sentence has to walk in, leading — the space between lines — is the oxygen in the room. Print gets away with 1.45; a backlit screen, with its soft halation, asks for more. The body text you are reading is set at 1.78: loose enough that the lines never blur into a gray ribbon, tight enough that a paragraph still reads as one thought and not a ladder.

Then there is the question of volume. A page with one size of type is a monotone; a page with twelve is a shouting match. The discipline is a scale — a small set of sizes, each a fixed ratio from the last, so that every heading, caption, and footnote is related to every other the way notes are related in a chord.2 Hierarchy done well is invisible; you only feel it as the sense that someone is speaking clearly.

Which brings us to the empty parts. Margins, paragraph breaks, the wide quiet around a pull quote — these are not the absence of design. They are design’s verbs. Whitespace is what a page does when it pauses for emphasis, and like any pause, it only lands if the material around it is worth pausing for.

IV.Code Is Prose

It is a small scandal that code — the most-read prose of the last fifty years — is so often set badly. A monospace face is the web’s handwriting, and a code block is a stanza: line breaks are meter, indentation is tone of voice, and naming things is characterization. Developers will squint at a misaligned brace the way an editor winces at a doubled space. It is the same instinct, wearing a different coat.

A well-set block of code is an act of hospitality. It says: someone was here before you, and they expected you. This page eats its own cooking — the bar currently crossing the top of your screen, and the red capital that opened this essay, are both set by the nineteen lines below.4

progress.css 
/* the two tricks this page is built on */

.progress {
  position: fixed;
  inset: 0 0 auto 0;
  height: 3px;
  background: linear-gradient(90deg, #2b45d8, #c22f1e);
  transform-origin: 0 50%;
  transform: scaleX(var(--read, 0));
}

p.lede::first-letter {
  float: left;
  font-size: 4.9em;    /* a door into the text */
  line-height: .76;
  padding: .04em .13em 0 0;
  font-family: "Didot", Georgia, serif;
  color: #c22f1e;      /* rubricate, v. — to set in red */
}

Steal them. View Source remains the web’s oldest and most generous reading tradition: every page ever published shipped with its own annotated manuscript tucked under the floorboards. The craft of reading on screens begins the moment you stop consuming pages and start taking them apart.

V.The Long Way Home

None of this makes a page slower, in the sense that matters. A long, well-set essay respects attention the way a narrow trail respects a walker: it asks for time, and it pays the time back with direction. The slow web is not a nostalgia project. It is the observation that some things read better when they are built by hand, set kindly, and allowed — unapologetically — to be long.

So: wide enough to breathe, narrow enough to follow. A voice in the headline, oxygen in the lines, red ink where red ink is earned. The rest is restraint — the hardest part of the craft, and the only part the reader ever feels.
